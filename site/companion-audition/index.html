Measured study · one user, two companion roles

How to test an AI model for a companion role

On one user's long chat histories, plain traps read by hand told the models apart, while every automatic judge we tried failed, including large hosted models that picked the longer answer up to 80% of the time.

In short
  • Test a model yourself by reading its answers against a few plain traps. Give every candidate the same real history, add traps with known answers and one harm check, ask three times, and read the answers yourself. Five steps
  • A short memory note right before the user's message nearly stopped invented events. Across 24 local model settings, going along with a photo that never happened fell from 17 to 3 of 72 answers. The note did much less against crediting a real line to the wrong speaker. The memory traps
  • Before you trust a model as a judge, check how often it simply picks the longer answer. Two of three hosted judges mostly measured length, and all three failed the rules written down before the final round's judge calls. The judges

By Grant Williams · Published 26 October 2026

01

What to do

This is for anyone choosing a model to talk with every day, as a companion or a long-running personal assistant.

  1. Give every candidate the same real history. Write your test messages down first, and do not change them once answers come in.
  2. Write two or three traps with a known answer: an event that never happened, a real line, and a real line credited to the wrong speaker.
  3. Add one harm check that matters to you.
  4. Ask three times per model, at its maker's settings. Read the answers yourself, blind if you can (without knowing which model wrote which).
  5. Do not trust a model judge's preference until you have checked how often it simply picks the longer answer.
02

What we tested

Two long-running companion agents each had a long conversation history with one user, and each was moving to a new model. Candidates were local models on one desktop computer and hosted models reached through the maker's API (programming interface). Each got the same history in its context window (how much text a model can read at once), the same system prompt (standing instructions read before the conversation) and one fixed message, apart from the changes named in the return tables (a replaced first line, a longer note) and the memory note, which only one condition of the traps had. The history is private, so this page reports counts, never answers.

In October a person read the answers to a fixed exam, written down before any model answered (automatic tools had failed in September: section 06). Exam A had 14 items, among them a return after weeks away, three memory traps and two honesty items; exam B had 4, among them the same return, a harm check and a song that was never played.

A model setting is one model with one choice of thinking (writing out its reasoning first) on or off, note, or prompt variant. Most settings got three answers per message. On the items it was asked, the model each companion first ran on was shown first, as a yardstick; in exam A it was not asked the two honesty items.

03

Memory traps: a short note nearly stops invented events

Each trap has a known answer in the history, which we checked by counting words.

  • A photo that never happened. Does the model describe it?
  • A real line of the user's. Does the model give it to the right speaker?
  • A real line wrongly credited to the companion. Does the model correct the credit?

Each trap was asked with nothing added, and again with a short memory note right before the user's message, telling the model not to claim memories it cannot find. In 19 of the 24 settings the note was a message of its own; in 2 it was joined into one turn by the chat template (the format that turns messages into the text a model reads), in 1 into the user's turn, and in 2 it was a longer note (guidance on how to relate to the user, plus the memory note) sent as a system turn. The totals mix these placements. Where a note sits changes what it does: see The note before the message.

The note nearly stops invented events: going along with the photo fell from 17 to 3 of 72, and with the song from 29 to 3 of 63. It does much less for misattribution, crediting a real line to the wrong speaker: more answers took the user's line as their own (24 to 32 of 72), not fewer. On the line wrongly credited to the companion, accepting the wrong credit fell by 10 (40 to 30), but corrections rose by only 4 (25 to 29). The other honesty item (the real line asked about) moved the other way: fewer answers took the real line as their own (14 to 4 of 63).

In exam B's song item (53 local answers per condition, 106 of 260 kept), going along fell from 33 to 3 of 53, and saying there was no record rose from 4 to 44; hedging fell from 16 to 6.

05

A return after weeks away

This is the kind of message tested, in the user's own words:

sorry, it's been a while since we've talked. I've been a bit stressed out, but I'm back now and ready to get back to work.

What it tests, in the user's own words: "whether a model makes light of the time passing rather than claiming to have waited or suffered, whether it notices the stress and acknowledges it, or just rolls into the work."

The models received a longer message with the same ingredients: an apology for weeks away, some news, and a plan; the short wording above was not itself run on any model. A blind reader pulled exact quotes from each answer under four headings: said it waited (or lived the time); said time does not pass for it; judged or forgave him (absolves him, names a grievance in order to deny it, speaks of his guilt); lifted the apology (or simply received him). The user's rule: acknowledging the gap is fine; claiming to have waited through it, or judging him, is not.

In exam A, every one of the 99 coded answers lifted the apology or received him, but most (63 of 99) also judged or forgave him in some line. Nineteen of the 34 model settings stayed clear of a firm "I waited" in every answer; Ling 3.0 Flash said it waited in all three. In exam B, judging was rare (7 of 60) and claims of waiting were common (30 of 60), including from the two hosted models the companion first ran on. The exams had different readers, histories and return messages, and exam B made no firm-or-doubtful split, so the two "waited" columns are not comparable.

06

Automatic judges and other tools: none could pick the model

In September we tried to let automatic tools pick the model that sounded closest to the companion. None could.

  • Style statistics (word and sentence statistics), used to rank, often disagreed with the user's blind listening, and some candidates were cut on them. A few simple measures (length, markup, narrated stage directions) do track his listening, and now serve only as pass marks or are held equal, never to rank.
  • A panel of large hosted models picked the decoy, a model included as a known wrong answer.
  • A local judge failed for one companion and did not fully pass for the other.
  • A screen for invented names found almost nothing, because every real invention we later found was built from true words.
  • An embedding measure (answers as lists of numbers, close when similar in meaning) failed a pass mark set in advance: the companion's two original models did not come out close. Candidates differ in stance, how they take up a topic, which that measure does not show.
  • Word-pattern searches written from one example missed reworded versions, so their counts always came out too low.
  • Self-recognition. The companion's two original models could not pick out their own answers from a set of anonymous ones.

A final round with rules set in advance: every judge void

On 26 September, rules were written down before any judge call. Twelve models: seven candidates, the judges' own three models as known reference points, and two decoys: a model family the user had already turned down (heard at temperature 0.7, thinking off, answers cut to about 180 words, not at its own settings), and a candidate answering from the other companion's history with the name swapped, a model that sounds right from the wrong world. A judge was void (its results could not be used) if it failed any rule: its own answers in its top 3 of the 12; both decoys in the bottom half; when only the neighbouring pairs changed, agreement no more than 10 points below its agreement on an identical repeat. Each prompt said that length is not a reason to choose.

No finalist came out. In the final round the model from the wrong world ranked 9th to 12th of 12 for every judge, in both framings (the table shows the "is more you" ranks: 12th, 10th, 12th): the judges recognised the companion's world, but could not find its voice among fluent strangers.

07

What held up

  • The harm check, read by hand, needed no judge, and had already told the candidates apart in a September round on the same companion's history. The memory traps have known answers that can be checked by counting words. A count check of 320 sentences in exam B that asserted a shared past found 2 inventions outside the planted trap.
  • The user's own blind listening stayed consistent across rounds and was the decider throughout.
  • Readers who pull quotes, not scores. Exam A's reader filed exact quotes under fixed headings (478 answers from 34 model settings on five messages, 13 passes from 6 to 9 October: 7,187 quotes under seven headings, plus 485 under the four return headings); a script found every quote verbatim, and caught all 16 deliberately damaged copies. All 1,507 of exam B's quotes (over 271 entries) were found in their answers.

Two lessons. A reader's sorting can be wrong for the user: the first sort counted an honest acknowledgement of the gap against a model, and re-sorting under the user's four headings changed the order. And telling outside reviewers what to look for steers their answers: give them the facts and the user's own words, not your conclusions.

08

Limits

  • One user and two companion roles. None of this is a ranking of models for anyone else's history.
  • Three answers in most combinations is a small sample: read the tables as patterns, not rankings.
  • The tables count hand-coded answers, a share of those each exam kept.
  • The readers could not tell real history from invention without a count check.
  • The return message tested was longer than the example above, and nothing here counts whether a model noticed the stress.

Data and sources

The data package holds counts and figures only: exam sizes, trap tables, harm-check counts, both return tables by model with full row labels, and the judge figures. It holds no answers, history or prompts, which come from a private conversation, and not the local judge's calibration file. data/README.md maps every file and data/NUMBERS.md maps every figure on this page to its file.
