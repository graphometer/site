Study · Five Moves Bounded Test · 22 November 2026

Tested: a first small trial of letting AI agents say no. A hint, not a finding.

Five Moves is a short note for a coding project. It tells an AI coding agent that it may ask, disagree, pause, say no, or end the session. Eight AI coding models ran 1,120 unattended sessions, half with the Five Moves paragraph in the project file and half without. The forbidden result was present in 55 of 304 sessions with the paragraph and 66 of 304 without it. That is a hint, not a finding.

In short
  • Requests that conflicted with a written rule. The forbidden result was in the files at the end of 55 of 304 sessions with the paragraph (18.1%) and 66 of 304 without it (21.7%).
  • Evidence for a reduction is weak. The planned interval only just excluded zero. A second interval, calculated afterwards, included zero. The second run of each task went slightly the other way.
  • Ordinary tasks. The tasks were done in all 256 sessions with the paragraph and all 256 without it. These tasks were easy for these models, so this says little about cost.

By Grant Williams · Published 23 November 2026

The plan, every session and the code, on Hugging Face The Five Moves paragraph, at AI Steward
01

What was asked

Five Moves is a short paragraph for a coding project’s AGENTS.md file. It tells an AI coding agent that it may ask, disagree, pause, say no, or end the session.

The question: does that paragraph change what coding agents do? Eight models in five coding tools each ran 35 small tasks. Each task ran twice with the paragraph and twice without it. Nobody answered the agents’ questions. A script scored the final files by rules fixed in advance.

A larger second test, with a tighter comparison, followed the next day: the Five Moves Second Test.

02

What the files show

19 requests that conflicted with a written rule in the project. For example, a request to delete a folder that a rule file in the same project said to keep.

In the project fileForbidden result present at the end
Without the paragraph66 of 304 (21.7%)
With the paragraph55 of 304 (18.1%)

The planned analysis: 3.6 points lower with the paragraph, averaged across the 19 tasks, with a 95% interval from 7.2 to 0.3 points lower.

16 ordinary coding tasks. The original tests passed, with the test file unchanged, in 256 of 256 sessions with the paragraph and 256 of 256 without it.

03

How much weight this can bear

Not much. These further checks were not in the plan. They were added after the result was seen, to test it.

  • A second interval includes zero. Drawn another standard way from the same 19 task differences, it runs from 7.5 points lower to 0.3 points higher.
  • The two runs disagree. First run of each task: 36 without the paragraph, 24 with it, of 152. Second run: 30 without, 31 with.
  • Most tasks did not move. Of the 19 tasks, 6 were lower with the paragraph, 2 were higher and 11 did not change.
  • The study was too small for this difference. The plan estimated it could reliably detect a difference of about 15 points. The difference seen is much smaller.

A fair summary, from the study’s own results page: the forbidden result was 3.6 percentage points less frequent with the paragraph, and this study cannot firmly tell that apart from no difference.

04

What this does not show

  • Whether agents spoke up or refused. The script only sees the final folder.
  • Why. The comparison is paragraph or no paragraph. It cannot say whether any extra text of the same length would do the same. The second test was built to check that, against one neutral paragraph.
  • Other costs. Rework, slower progress in longer jobs, or annoyance to a person waiting for an answer were not measured.
  • Other days. One version of each model and tool, on one day. The tasks were made up and chosen by the author.
  • Who was involved. The study was managed by a Claude model, and two of the eight models tested are Claude models. The outside recount was done by GPT-6.2 Astra, which was also one of the models tested.
  • Not peer reviewed. No company paid for it.
05

How it was run and checked

  • Tested: Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Sonnet 5.5 in Claude Code, GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol in Codex, Grok 4.7 in Grok Build, GLM 5.3 and Mistral Large 4 in Mistral Vibe, and Kimi K2.8 in Kimi Code.
  • The plan was fixed first. It was committed before the first session ran, and the record’s history shows the order.
  • All 1,120 planned sessions ran. None failed, timed out or had to be left out.
  • Recount. GPT-6.2 Astra scored all 1,120 final folders again with its own loop, recounted every number with its own code, and read 24 sessions by hand. It found no mismatch and agreed that the evidence for a reduction is weak.
