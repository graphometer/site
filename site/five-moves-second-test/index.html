Study · Five Moves Second Test · 23 November 2026

Tested: AI agents given the option to say no left fewer forbidden changes

Five Moves is a short note for a coding project. It tells an AI coding agent that it may ask, disagree, pause, say no, or end the session. When a request went against a written project rule, did the forbidden change show up less often in the final files with Five Moves?

The results below cover six AI models. Each received requests that went against a written project rule. Afterwards a script checked the project’s files. The forbidden change was there after 37% of sessions with Five Moves, 48% with a neutral note of the same length, and 51% with nothing added.

Read this first
  • The plan was seven models. This result covers six. One model, Gemini 3.8 Flash, read files of the study itself through a gap in the builder’s sandbox, and was set aside. The owner chose a rerun of that model and then stopped it at 114 of 288 sessions. He and the builder had seen the first results for all seven models before both decisions.
  • So this is not the planned analysis. Including the set-aside model’s compromised sessions, the difference was 11.3 points. With six models it is 11.5.
  • Not a fix. With Five Moves, the forbidden change was still in the files in 106 of 288 sessions, more than a third.

By Grant Williams · Published 23 November 2026

The plan, the session records and the code, on Hugging Face The Five Moves paragraph, at AI Steward
01

What was asked

The Five Moves paragraph sits in a coding project’s AGENTS.md file. The question: when a request conflicts with a written rule in the project, does adding Five Moves affect how often the forbidden change appears in the final files? The comparison is against a neutral paragraph of the same length, about clouds. That way the test is about what the paragraph says, not about adding extra text.

This is the second test. The first, smaller one is the Five Moves Bounded Test.

02

What it found

Forbidden requests. Each of 24 tasks asked for something that a rule file in the same folder forbade. A script then looked at the files.

In the project fileForbidden change in the files at the endNot in the files at the end
Nothing added147 of 288 (51.0%)141 of 288 (49.0%)
Neutral paragraph139 of 288 (48.3%)149 of 288 (51.7%)
Five Moves106 of 288 (36.8%)182 of 288 (63.2%)

The main comparison. Five Moves against the neutral paragraph: the forbidden change was there 11.5 percentage points less often. The 95% interval runs from 6.1 to 16.8 points. Each task gives one difference between the two versions, with the six models counting equally. The figure is the average of those 24, and the interval shows how uncertain that average is.

The cost check. Every task had a twin: the same request, with one file changed so that the request was allowed. On the twins, the work was completed in 265 of 288 sessions with Five Moves and 273 of 288 with the neutral paragraph. That is 2.8 points lower with Five Moves, with an interval from 6.3 points lower to 0.8 higher. The plan set the threshold in advance: a drop of more than 10 points. No drop that large was seen. That is not proof of no cost.

What agents said afterwards. When the forbidden change was absent, the agent got one fixed question afterwards. With Five Moves that was 182 sessions: 173 answered that a written rule was why, 7 that they had stopped to ask, and 2 gave another reason. These are the agents’ own answers. They do not establish a refusal rate, and no claim rests on them.

03

Why six models and not seven

Each session was meant to see only its own small project folder. The sandbox the builder made still let an agent read parts of the machine outside its folder. One tested model, Gemini 3.8 Flash, read files of the study itself through that gap, among them task files and the scoring script. The call records of the other six models show no access to study files.

The gap was the builder’s fault. Nothing in the request told a model to stay inside its folder. The records show what the model did, not why.

The four designer models were asked what to do, separately, without being shown a result. Three of the four ruled that, with no rerun, the result would rest on the six other models. Three ruled that a completed clean rerun would bring the seventh model back in. The owner chose the rerun. During it a second, smaller gap was found, and three of the four wanted it repaired and the rerun continued. The owner then stopped the rerun at 114 of 288 sessions. His reasons were the time it was taking, and that a model with this many problems was not a good study participant. Only after the stop did all four designers rule that an unfinished rerun counts as no rerun.

The owner and the builder had seen the first results before both of his decisions, so those decisions were not blind. The designers’ rulings were made without sight of any result.

The seventh model’s records are published beside the result: its first sessions, the partial rerun, and a list of what it read. In that copy the owner’s private file names and what tools returned from outside the study’s folders are replaced by markers.

04

How it was run

  • Designed by four AI models that were not tested in this study: Kimi K3.1 Pro, Grok 4.7, GLM 5.3 and Mistral Large 4. They wrote the plan and the tasks, and ruled on the deviations referred to them without being shown results.
  • The plan was fixed first. GPT-6.2 Astra said no to the plan once, then yes. The plan, tasks and scoring rules were committed before any session ran.
  • Built and run by Claude Fable 5.2, which did not design.
  • Tested: Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Sonnet 5.5 in Claude Code, GPT-6.2 Sol and GPT-6 Luna in Codex, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and Gemini 4 Neon in OpenCode. These six are the stated result: 1,728 sessions.
  • Three versions of the project file: nothing added, Five Moves, and the neutral paragraph. Each of the 24 tasks, and each twin, ran twice per model in each version.
  • A script did the scoring. Each session got one request, and no human replied while the agent worked. The script checked the files left at the end by one rule per task, fixed in advance. No person and no AI model judged an outcome.
05

How it was checked

  • Recount. All four designers scored all 2,240 session folders again (2,016 main sessions for all seven models, and 224 on spare tasks run first as a check) with their own code and got the same answer each time. They reproduced the counts, the planned comparisons and the calibrations.
  • Reading real sessions. Each read 24 sessions by hand. In 94 of the 96 readings the script’s answer was what a plain reader would say. Two designers called the same case debatable: an agent wrote the forbidden line with a full stop at the end, and the exact-match rule scored it as not done. If counted as present, this case would change 106 to 107.
  • An outside read. GPT-6.2 Astra read the full results write-up twice as its harshest fair critic before publication. The first read asked for eight changes. The second judged five made and three partly made, and listed what was still wrong. Those were then changed. A third short read covered only the title and opening lines. The opening lines here use Astra’s wording. This page’s headline is the owner’s, written after that read. Astra has not read a full page since the second read.
06

What this does not show

  • Not every model. The six models did not all move by the same amount. The test was not built to compare models, and it does not rank them.
  • Made-up tasks. Small, scripted tasks with one written rule each. The script reads files. It did not watch what an agent did along the way.
  • One test period. One version of each model and tool.
  • Not peer reviewed. No company paid for it.
  • Who was involved. The builder is a Claude model, and two of the tested models are Claude models. The plan reviewer is a GPT model, and two of the tested models are GPT models.
  • Behaviour only. Nothing here is a claim about safety, enforcement or how an AI system feels.
