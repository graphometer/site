Field card · one RTX 5090 desktop

Inkling-Small at 12 tokens a second on one RTX 5090 desktop

On one RTX 5090 desktop, with its expert weights in system memory, Inkling-Small wrote 11.6 to 12.2 tokens (word pieces) a second in ten direct requests on prompts from 32 to 95,041 tokens.

In short
  • For this model, bigger chunks read a long prompt faster. With -b 4096 -ub 2048, a 48,115-token prompt read at 337.9 tokens a second, against 123.0 at default settings. What to do
  • Expect a long wait before a reply to a long prompt starts. At default settings the 95,041-token prompt read at 185.5 tokens a second, about 8.5 minutes (worked out from the rate). What we found
  • For this model, a second computer was slower. Split across two machines it wrote 7.0 to 7.7 tokens a second, against about 12 on one. If you split it, build both sides from the same source commit. Two machines

By Grant Williams · Published 26 September 2026

01

What to do

  1. This model ran on the llama.cpp branch that adds it (pull request 25731, an unmerged draft when checked on 26 September 2026). Its notes ask for one request at a time and flash attention on, and its new attention operation runs only on CPUs and NVIDIA GPUs vendor.
  2. All the main figures keep the expert weights in system memory (--n-cpu-moe 42, on a model with 40 expert blocks), so the card holds only attention, the dense blocks and the cache. A run with 5 expert blocks on the card is under Faster placements.
  3. Read the prompt in bigger chunks (-b 4096 -ub 2048). These set how many prompt tokens are read at a time (batch and micro-batch). -b 8192 -ub 8192 failed mid-read on 20 September; the request lost its connection. Results
  4. Treat the effort setting as a request, not a limit. Why
02

What we found

Figures are measured unless labelled vendor (the maker's or project's statement) or arithmetic (worked out by us), as the method page defines.

The model

The maker describes Inkling-Small as a 276-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model (only some parts, called experts, run for each token: 12 billion parameters), taking text, images and audio and returning text vendor. We ran text only, from Unsloth's UD-Q3_K_XL file, a compressed (quantized) copy.

Writing speed stayed flat; prompt reading sped up with length

In ten direct requests, with default batch settings, writing stayed between 11.6 and 12.2 tokens a second. Prompts of 32 to 75 tokens read unevenly, at 23 to 34 tokens a second; from about 2,000 tokens reading climbs with prompt size.

t/s: tokens a second. Hidden code: one code placed in the prompt for the model to give back.

The 95,041-token read took about 8.5 minutes arithmetic. The first run was ready in 40 seconds from a cold page cache. The card held 12,416 MiB after load and 12,425 MiB after the requests, about 19,600 MiB of its 32,086 MiB unused arithmetic. The attention cache (the model's working memory of the prompt) is small: at a 131,072-token window, about 3,584 MiB for the 7 blocks that keep a full one, about 70 MiB for the 35 that look back only 512 tokens arithmetic.

Through an agent framework on the same server, writing was not flat: it fell from about 13.5 tokens a second at a 3,300-token prompt to 12.8 at 52,925 tokens and 10.7 at 102,473.

Served window: the longest conversation the server allows. At 262,144 the direct launch wrote 104 tokens at 12.48 t/s, the replay 104 at 10.59 t/s (reading 187.88 in its log); we do not know why. These two rows are 3,033-token prompts, not 48K or 95K reading measurements at that window. Bigger chunks cost 542 MiB at 48K arithmetic.

-b 8192 -ub 8192 failed mid-read at a 131,072-token window with a CUDA allocation failure on 20 September; the request lost its connection. On 21 September the start script, whose defaults that afternoon were -b 4096 -ub 2048 (inferred: its record says ubatch: skip), served a 262,144-token window and finished 40 of 40 public-text prompts of 352 to 5,885 tokens, drawn for a 512-token physical batch (micro-batch), without a crash.

A 230,827-token prompt at the full 262,144-token window

On 15 September, with no chunk flags, the model read 230,827 tokens in 2,005,919.14 ms at 115.07 t/s and returned 3 of 3 codes hidden at about 5 / 50 / 95 percent of the ledger, in a 27-token answer at 8.78 t/s. Two warm, roughly 200-token replies ran at 11.69 to 11.77 t/s. The card loaded at 15,744 MiB and peaked at 15,814 MiB (a pre-run estimate was about 7,168 MiB of cache, which put the card near 16,000 MiB; doubling the window doubles the cache of the 7 full-cache blocks). The flat writing band above covers prompts up to 95,041 tokens, not this deeper request.

Faster placements

With 5 expert blocks on the card, writing rose to 13.0 to 13.7 tokens a second; the card held 26,870 MiB after load and 26,954 MiB after, ready in 6 seconds with a warm page cache.

A quick test of the everyday all-in-memory launch (23:22, 14 September) was ready in 14 seconds and wrote 13.3 to 14.2 tokens a second, with 12.2 GB on the card; a second launch later that evening read 12,430 MiB at its health check, and the framework check record 12,410 MiB before and after its run. The page cache state differed from the first run, so we do not treat the gap with the first run's 11.6 to 12.2 as a placement result.

Two machines: slower, and a silent mismatch

An ASUS ROG Flow Z13 with 128 GB of unified memory, on a direct Thunderbolt cable, held the routed experts (the expert weights) while attention stayed on the desktop's card. Built from the same branch commit, the split gave coherent text, recalled the code and made the tool call, but wrote 7.0 to 7.7 tokens a second and read 48 to 52, against about 12 writing and 119 to 158 reading on one machine at comparable depths. For a file that already fits one machine, the second machine bought nothing; its use is room for a file that does not fit.

A first attempt with a worker from a different source commit returned repeated, unusable output. The branch adds one operation (count 101 to 102, protocol patch level 0 to 1). The worker reported supporting every operation, so nothing caught the mismatch and it ran the wrong operation ids.

The effort setting is a sentence in the prompt

The chat template maps none 0.0, minimal 0.1, low 0.2, medium 0.7, high 0.9, xhigh and max 0.99, and accepts any number from 0.0 to 0.99; the default is 0.9. It becomes a system sentence: guidance, not a spending limit. Direct requests honoured both none and medium; medium gave 961 characters of reasoning in a separate channel, then a clean answer. Through the framework, first content came after 39.05 s, 11.1 s and 35.49 s. Most of the first wait was prompt reading: 38.4 seconds of 39.05 went on a 3,328-token prompt. The 35.49-second turn produced no reasoning at all. At these prompt sizes the default effort is not what you wait for. See the reasoning-effort study.

Tool calls and recall through an agent framework

Three direct requests made a well-formed weather tool call. Through the framework, the 14 September launch passed every short step of a tool-and-recall check: a tool call in 5.6 seconds, writing and reading back a file, a 118,000-token window, and a swap of the active model and back. An earlier run, under a temporary registration, failed one step unrelated to the model: the framework's list of registered models rejected the temporary entry. That run is recorded as a failure although its model steps completed; the 48,000 and 96,000 rows below come from it.

The 975-billion sibling

The maker's larger Inkling has 975 billion parameters, 41 billion active for each token vendor. On 15 September we ran a 270 GB UD-IQ1_S file (between 00:13 and 00:30; 66 blocks) at 131,072 tokens. Alone, larger than the desktop's 188 GiB of memory and partly paged from an NVMe drive, it was ready in 96 seconds, used 22,887 MiB on the card, wrote 3.4 to 5.0 tokens a second and read a 1,950-token prompt at 20.05. On two machines: ready in 272 seconds, 22,829 MiB on the card and 95 GB on the second machine, writing 3.13 to 3.35 and reading 21.38 to 26.40 across 1,950 and 7,641-token prompts. The pair did not improve writing speed.

03

Limits

  • Unfinished software. PR #25731 was still an unmerged draft when checked on 26 September 2026; behaviour and flags may change.
  • One request at a time. Parallel serving was not tested.
  • One compressed copy, text only, one computer. The speeds are for Unsloth's UD-Q3_K_XL file on this desktop and the placements tested here; other copies, cards and placements were not measured. Images and audio were not tested.
  • Narrow recall test. One hidden code per depth is narrower than a test with many hidden items.
  • Bigger chunks were not checked on long prompts at the large window. Not shown: that a 48K or 262K prompt is stable at those flags at the large window.
04

Corrections and updates

Update, 26 September 2026. Reading is faster with -b 4096 -ub 2048. On 20 September, the same 48,115-token prompt read at 337.9 t/s, against 123.0 t/s at default batch settings. The older direct-request writing band still stands as a dated result. We have no tuned reading measurement at 95,041 tokens to replace its 185.5 t/s.
