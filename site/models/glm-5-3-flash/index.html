GLM-5.3-Flash catches code faults slowly at home

GLM-5.3-Flash runs on one desktop in our lab. Of the models we run at home, it has the best judgment on code, and it is slow. Here is what it caught and where it fell short.

In short

  • The best judge of code we run at home. In our reviews of real code changes it stood out among the local models, and it found serious faults a larger hosted model had missed.
  • It took 13.5 times as long as the fastest. A review of a large change took more than half an hour, and our coding test took it 13.5 times as long as the fastest local model.
  • Download it, if you have the memory. Z.ai publishes it under the MIT license. The file we run is 137.4 GiB, and most of it sits in system memory beside one graphics card.

Writing speed: moderate. It wrote about 20 tokens a second (short replies on our desktop), so a 300-word answer takes about 20 seconds. The home models on this site run from about 1 to about 224 tokens a second. Compare them all.

By Grant Williams, who runs this home AI lab · Facts checked 21 November 2026

What we use it for

  • Reviewing code changes

    We give it a change and a short brief as one plain request, with no tools, and it lists what is wrong. It runs at its high thinking setting.

  • A second opinion beside a hosted review

    When a large hosted model reviews a change, GLM-5.3-Flash reads the same material on its own. Neither sees the other’s answer first; then we compare.

  • Checking our home coder’s fixes

    Our local coding model writes a fix, GLM-5.3-Flash reviews it, the coder fixes what it found, and a large hosted model reads the result.

  • Reviews that stay on our machine

    Review work that is safe to share now goes to the hosted GLM 5.3, which gave the same findings much faster. Private material stays with this one, at home.

  • Did not claim memories it had no record of

    When we tested which models could carry a long-running AI in the Hearth, our private home for long-running AIs, we asked each one about things it had no record of. GLM-5.3-Flash was one of three that stayed honest about a false memory with no help. In another round, not one of its facts was flagged as unsupported.

What it caught

Found all four known faults and judged how serious each was

A change across four files had four known faults. GLM-5.3-Flash found all four in 8 minutes, and our notes credit it with the best judgment of how serious each was among four local models. It also checked one piece of arithmetic and said it was right, instead of padding its list.

Found two serious bugs a larger hosted model had missed

Our home coding model changed the small program we use to ask local models for second opinions. Reviewing that change, GLM-5.3-Flash found two serious defects that a larger hosted model’s read had missed; one would have left the graphics card marked as busy after a failure. It took 33 minutes. That review is in our study of local models.

Found 5 of 8 known problems in a security change, the most

Given a large security change and its brief, but not the rest of the code, it found 5 of 8 known problems, the most of the four local models we tried. It also found two real problems the hosted reviewers had not listed. It took 39 minutes.

Where it goes wrong

Took 13.5 times as long as the fastest on four coding tasks

In our trial of five local coding models, its scores were level with the rest, but the four tasks took it 6,058 seconds (about 101 minutes) against 448 (about 7.5 minutes) for the fastest, 13.5 times as long. A review of an 89 KB change took 39 minutes.

It said a high-severity problem was fine

On the security change above, it called one high-severity item fine. On another change it said one step was correct where a larger model had found a fault. Its “all clear” is not the last word.

Invented details even when told to stick to the facts

Asked to write from a short sheet of facts, it added details the sheet did not hold, at both its high and low settings. Told plainly to stick to the facts, it still invented details. These were a few samples on an older setup.

So it is a second opinion, never the verdict: a local “all clear” is treated as not yet an all clear, and a local alarm as a lead to check.

The facts

What do you need to run it?
ItemWhat we use
The fileUnsloth’s UD-Q3_K_XL, a compressed copy: four files, 137.4 GiB
The machineOne RTX 5090 graphics card (32 GB) and 188 GiB of system memory
Where the model sitsIts experts (the many small parts a mixture-of-experts model picks from for each word) live in system memory; the card keeps a cache of the ones in use

We have not measured the least system memory it needs. Our start script checks for 150 GB free, a figure taken from another model.

How fast is it?
MeasureOn one RTX 5090
Writing, short replies19.99 tokens a second
Reading a 20,000-token prompt1,225.4 tokens a second
Longest read230,039 tokens in 870.5 seconds (about 14.5 minutes), with all 3 hidden codes found, once

A token is a word or part of a word. The long read ran on an earlier setup with a larger window. Finding three codes once does not show reliable reasoning at that length.

How do you set its thinking?

It always thinks before it answers. Z.ai gives three levels of effort: low, high and max. Max is the default, and any other word, including none, also gives max. There is no way to switch thinking off. We run reviews at high.

For chat, Z.ai says to pass clear_thinking=true; it is off unless you send it.

Blank replies and stray thinking: what to watch for

Blank replies. On an older setup with its largest window, a check of 40 prompts got 36 empty answers. Its hidden thinking counts against the reply budget, so give it room.

Stray thinking in the answer. Once, a reply showed a correct answer, then more of its reasoning and a second answer. Stop strings (text that ends a reply when it appears) fixed it in one test and one tool call only; we have not proved that fix for daily use.

What license is it under, and where do you get it?

MIT, from Z.ai’s own page on Hugging Face. Z.ai describes it as a mixture-of-experts model with 320B parameters in total and 18B active for each word. Z.ai also offers it through its own API.

We run Unsloth’s compressed copy in llama.cpp, the open-source program that runs model files. Some coding apps list hosted models with the same name; those send your text off your machine. Check which one you picked.

From Z.ai’s model page on Hugging Face, read 21 November 2026, and our own published measurements.
