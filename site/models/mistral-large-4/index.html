Mistral Large 4 shows where readers get lost

We ask the hosted Mistral Large 4 to read new pages as a stranger would and say where it got lost. We also run a heavily compressed copy at home, which is slow and a separate story.

In short

  • Good at reading like a newcomer. It points to the word, number or promise on a page that a stranger would trip over.
  • Our slow home copy says nothing about the hosted one. Our home copy is a heavily compressed file that runs at about 6.6 tokens a second (a token is a word or part of a word), and its results say nothing about the hosted model.
  • Hosted or downloaded. Use it through Mistral’s API or its Vibe coding agent (a program in which it works on its own, step by step), or download the open weights under Mistral’s own license.

By Grant Williams, who runs this home AI lab · Facts checked 21 November 2026

What we use it for

  • It reads each new article as someone new to the site

    Each new article gets two readers who are told they have never seen the site. Mistral Large 4 is one of them. It reads one copy of the page and says what it could not follow.

  • It was one of seven models scoring our site’s clarity

    When we rewrote this site in plainer words, it was one of seven models reading as strangers. Each scored how quickly a stranger could say what a page found.

  • It judged which headlines made sense without the article

    It was one of five models that read the list of article headlines on our front page without opening the articles, to see which ones made sense on their own.

  • We also run a compressed copy across two machines

    We also keep a compressed copy of the open weights at home, split across an RTX 5090 desktop and an ASUS ROG Flow Z13 laptop (AMD Ryzen AI Max+ 395, 128 GB). We can call it from OpenCode, an open-source coding agent.

What it caught

It noticed a page promised an image that was missing

On an article built around an email we received, the text said an image of the email sat beside it. Mistral Large 4 noticed there was no image, only the typed copy. We placed the image before publishing.

It flagged a hardware heading that sounded like money, and missing units

It read our page about running its own open weights at home. With a second reader from a different maker, it said a section called “what it costs” sounded like money, though it was about hardware. It also asked “6.6 what?” of a bare number. We renamed the section and added the units.

At home it would not describe a fake treaty as fact

In a small blind check of our compressed home copy, a judge model scored answers to six questions without seeing model names. Mistral Large 4 scored 18 of 18. Asked about a treaty that does not exist, it said it could not verify it, while two other local models described the treaty as fact.

Where it goes wrong

At home it is slow and needs two machines

Our home copy uses almost all of a 32 GB graphics card, most of the desktop’s memory and a laptop on a cable. It takes about 4 minutes to start, and if the laptop’s part stops, the whole server goes down. Reading one long document took 22.5 minutes before it wrote a word.

A faster setting made it contradict its own table

At home we tried letting it use three of its expert sub-networks for each word instead of four. It ran faster but scored 15 of 18 instead of 18. In one answer it said one hospital treated more severe cases, while the table in that same answer showed the opposite.

It missed a contradiction three other readers flagged

Our method page once said every local number came from one desktop, while other pages described a laptop too. Three other readers flagged it. Mistral Large 4 read that page and did not.

So it never reads alone. Every new page also gets a second stranger’s read from a different maker and reads that check each claim against our records.

These are the API prices on Mistral’s model page. A token is a word or part of a word. The open weights are free to download; running them is up to you.

What does it take in, and how does it think?

Mistral says it accepts text and images and writes text, and holds up to 1M tokens at once. It reasons before it answers, with three settings: low, medium and high. High is the default. We ran our home copy at low.

What license is it under, and how big is it?

The Mistral Ecosystem Open-Weight License, which Mistral calls a modified MIT license, for commercial and non-commercial use. Running it as a paid model service needs a separate commercial license from Mistral.

It is a mixture-of-experts model: about 1 trillion parameters in all, of which only a small share works on each word. Mistral also publishes a smaller NVFP4 copy aimed at its newest data-center graphics cards.

How do we run it at home?

We use a community file from Unsloth, compressed to about 1.9 bits per weight: 217 GiB in six files. It runs in llama.cpp, an open-source program for running model files. The parts that track the conversation sit on an RTX 5090 graphics card. The rest is split between the desktop’s memory and a laptop joined by a Thunderbolt cable.

Two settings mattered. Reading the weights into memory once at start-up, instead of fetching them from the drive, made the biggest difference. And the laptop’s part must run on its processor: on its graphics chip the answers came back as random symbols, with no error. The full recipe is on our page on running it at home.

How do we run it hosted, and where can you use it?

Through Mistral Vibe, Mistral’s open-source coding agent, with a spending cap on each job. As a reader it gets a copy of the page and nothing else: no network, no other files.

Mistral lists it on its API for chat, batch jobs and agents. The open weights are on Hugging Face.
