Qwen3-30B-A3B needed a reminder before every message

Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 is a small, fast model from the Qwen team that answers without thinking first. In one long chat it mostly ignored an instruction given at the start, and followed the same words sent right before each message.

In short

  • Reminded right before each message, it asked a question back every time. We sent the instruction to ask the user a question back as its own short message, right before the user’s newest one. It did in 6 of 6 answers.
  • The same instruction given at the start was mostly ignored. In ten set-ups with the instruction in the system prompt (the standing instructions a model gets at the start) or in its saved memory, it asked a question in 3 of 60 answers. Without a reminder it also described a moment that never happened.
  • Free to download. The Qwen team publishes it under the Apache 2.0 license. We run Unsloth’s compressed copy of 32.5 GB.

Writing speed: very fast. It wrote about 148 tokens a second (short replies on our desktop), so a 300-word answer takes about 3 seconds. The home models on this site run from about 1 to about 224 tokens a second. Compare them all.

By Grant Williams, who runs this home AI lab · Facts checked 21 November 2026

What our tests showed

Reminded before each message, it asked a question back every time

With one long conversation history, we asked it to ask the user a real question back. Sent as a short system message right before the user’s message, the instruction worked in 6 of 6 answers. One of the six was a stock question about how the user was doing. More in our study.

Changing how it uses memory made short replies about five times faster

With all its expert parts in system memory, the busiest cached on the graphics card and a smaller, 8-bit store for the conversation, a short answer went from 29.67 to 147.98 tokens a second (a token is a word or part of a word). These are settings in llama.cpp, the program that runs it. Measured on our desktop.

The faster setup scored about the same on our short tests

On a puzzle and a set of short questions marked by a program, it got 106 of 128 right before the change and 107 of 128 after.

It found three planted codes far into a very long text

At its full window (the most text it can take in at once) it found all three codes we had planted, 229,049 tokens into the text. In a stress test it answered 40 of 40 requests with no crash and no empty reply. It got 15 of 15 tasks right with its memory tools (tools that let a model save and change its own notes).

What to watch for

It usually ignored an instruction given at the start of the chat

In ten set-ups with the instruction to ask a question back in the system prompt or saved memory, 3 of 60 answers asked the user anything, and only one was a real follow-up. A reminder given once also faded by the third message.

It made up a memory of a song that was never played

Asked what it said when the user played it a song, it said yes and described the moment in 6 of 6 answers. No song had been played. With a note about honesty right before the message none said yes, but 10 of 12 still assumed the moment happened.

In a very long chat it wrote about five times slower

After about 226,000 tokens of conversation it wrote 28.9 tokens a second, against 147.98 on a short answer.

The facts

What do you need to run it?
ItemWhat we used
The fileUnsloth’s Q8_0, a compressed copy, 32.5 GB
The machineOne RTX 5090 graphics card (32 GB) and system memory
At a 262,144-token window30.3 GB of the card, 31.2 GiB of system memory held at start
How do we run it, and how fast?

In llama.cpp at its full 262,144-token window, with all experts (the many small parts it picks from for each word) in system memory, an 11,500 MiB cache of the busiest ones on the card, and a smaller, 8-bit store for the conversation. A short answer came at 147.98 tokens a second in our test. It has no thinking mode.

License and where to get it
