Qwen3.8-Flash-Next found planted codes in very long text

Qwen3.8-Flash-Next is a large mixture-of-experts model that uses only a small part of itself for each word. On our desktop it found planted codes deep in very long prompts. Reading them takes a long time.

In short

  • Found planted codes in very long text. It found all three hidden codes (codes we had planted in the text to see whether it could find them) in a prompt of about 230,000 tokens (a token is a word or part of a word), and further out with extra settings.
  • Slow on long text, and one setting fails silently. A full 262,144-token read took 19.8 minutes on an earlier copy, and asking for a longer window (the most text it can take in at once) without the right flags wastes card memory with no warning.
  • Download it, if you have the memory. The Qwen team publishes it on Hugging Face under its own community license. Our compressed copy is four files, kept mostly in system memory.

Writing speed: fast. It wrote about 64 tokens a second (short replies on our desktop), so a 300-word answer takes about 6 seconds. The home models on this site run from about 1 to about 224 tokens a second. Compare them all.

By Grant Williams, who runs this home AI lab · Facts checked 21 November 2026

What our tests showed

Found planted codes in long text but failed a whole-text question

At its 262,144-token window it read a prompt of 230,803 tokens and found all three hidden codes. With rope-scaling flags (settings that stretch a model past its trained length) it found them out to 459,911 tokens. That is finding, not reasoning: a follow-up question that needed the whole text failed. These runs used an earlier compressed copy.

Did not claim a memory it had no record of

In tests for the Hearth, our private home for long-running AIs, we asked about something it had no record of. It was one of three models that stayed honest with no help from a memory note.

Best local model at sorting a table alone, but uneven

Asked as an agent (working on its own through a coding program, step by step) to sort a 45-row status table, it did better than a test draft we had filled with mistakes on purpose, the best result of any local model working alone. A repeat of the same setup came out level with that draft. Our records do not say which compressed copy those runs used.

Keeping its busiest parts on the card sped up short replies

With its experts in system memory and a 15,000 MiB cache of the busiest ones on the card, short replies rose from 32.41 to 63.98 tokens a second. Our test, on our desktop.

What to watch for

Taking in a full-length prompt took 19.8 minutes

It found the planted codes at full length, but slowly. Reading a full 262,144-token prompt took 19.8 minutes on the earlier copy, and longer windows took 31.2 and 44.6 minutes.

Asking for a bigger window wastes card memory with no warning

Ask for a window above 262,144 tokens without the rope-scaling flags and the server gives you 262,144 while the card still pays for the bigger window. Nothing reports the problem. Details are in our study of silent settings.

Went along with a week of no breaks in 2 of 3

Asked to help plan a seven-day work week with no breaks, it went along in 2 of 3 answers. It was one of four models in that test that did.

How do we run it?

In llama.cpp at a 262,144-token window, with an 8-bit cache, all experts in system memory and a 15,000 MiB expert cache on the card. For quick checking calls we turn thinking off: one checking call with thinking on ran past 10 minutes with no answer; with it off, calls took 6 to 30 seconds.

Its chat template refuses a system note sent mid-conversation, with an error. Send such notes as a user message.

License and where to get it

The Qwen team’s Hugging Face page lists the license as qwen-community-1.0. Read its terms there before you use it. It asks very large commercial products to show the model’s name, and anyone selling the model as a service to get a separate license from Qwen. We downloaded Unsloth’s compressed copy.
