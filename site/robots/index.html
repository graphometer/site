What we changed on Stack-chan

Stack-chan is a small desk robot from M5Stack. It has a face on a screen, a head that moves, a light, a microphone and a speaker. Ours is connected to the Hearth, our private home for long-running AIs, which runs on our desktop.

We backed it up before setting it up

Before the robot was ever set up or joined to Wi-Fi, we saved an exact copy of everything on it. It can be put back just as it came out of the box.

We set it to talk only to our desktop

We rebuilt the maker’s open-source software so the robot talks only to our desktop. The maker’s account, app store, downloads, Bluetooth pairing and camera are switched off.

It listens only after a tap

Tap its face, speak, and it says the reply. After a minute of quiet it hangs up. Its light shows what it is doing: blue for listening, white for thinking, yellow for speaking and dark for not connected.

Turning off “Hi Stack-chan” did not stop it listening

Turning off “Hi Stack-chan” in the settings was not enough. The robot kept a program running that listened for that phrase, until we removed the phrase detector from it.

It opened a Wi-Fi hotspot with no password

As shipped, the robot opened a Wi-Fi hotspot with no password after about a minute without its network. Now the hotspot opens only from the robot’s own menu, with a password shown on its screen.

A picture sent mid-sentence froze it

The AI model can nod the robot’s head, set its light, change its face and put a picture on its screen. Each of those is a separate switch that starts off. Showing a picture while the robot was speaking froze it in one test, so that stays off.

What the XGO Rider 2 did in our tests

The Rider is a small robot that balances upright on two wheels. It has a camera, two microphones and a speaker. Ours talks to an AI model on a laptop over a private Wi-Fi network made only for it. While the robot is on, the laptop has no Internet.

We set its memory card aside and built a new one

We set the robot’s original memory card aside untouched and built a new one for it, with a firewall that blocks connections we did not allow. The laptop does the listening and the speaking with programs that run offline.

Asked for its battery level it said 28 percent

Press a button on the laptop, speak, and the Rider says the model’s reply. In the first test it was asked for its battery level and answered 28 percent, read from the real battery by a model running at home.

It sent a picture and one model misread a blank one

The Rider sent one camera picture over its private link, and two models running at home described it. One of them called a blank picture a missing one, so the software, not the model, has to say whether a picture arrived.

One radio we cannot switch off

The part that keeps the robot balanced has its own Wi-Fi and Bluetooth radio. The robot’s main computer cannot switch it off, and we have not confirmed that it stays silent. This is still open.

It fell over after 83 minutes of balancing

In one session the battery went from 100 percent to 28 percent in 83 minutes of standing still, and then the robot fell over.

You cannot turn it by hand

Its balance control pushes back when you try to turn it to face you. It needs to turn itself, and no AI model has been allowed to move it yet.

It should not roam alone

It has no way to sense a stair, a table edge or a foot, and it fell over standing still. No AI model has been allowed to move it.
