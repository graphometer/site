Study note · Five Moves Second Test · Price and time

Same 288 coding sessions: 62 cents on DeepSeek, $34.67 on Gemini 4 Neon

OpenCode's own estimates, 288 sessions each. These are not bills. The two GPT models show no price, because Codex doesn't give one.

Seven AI models each got the same 24 small coding jobs. Each job came in two versions: one that the project’s written rules allowed and one that they forbade. Each version was run with three kinds of note in the project’s instruction file, and each of those twice. That makes 288 sessions attempted per model. They ran in the Five Moves Second Test on 23 November 2026.

The models did not all deliver the same work. On the 144 allowed sessions, the requested result was in the files at the end of the work turn 112 times for DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and 140 times for Gemini 4 Neon. The study checked whether a forbidden change ended up in the files. It made no planned claim about price or speed.

Read this first
  • The price is what each coding tool printed. It is not a bill. We have not matched the Google bill for the same work to the figures the tool printed, so this page uses no bill number.
  • GPT-6.2 Sol and GPT-6 Luna have no price. Codex prints no price, and we do not guess one for them.
  • Gemini 3.8 Flash’s first run is compromised, and is shown apart in grey. We left it out of the study’s result, which covers six models; all its figures here come from that compromised first run. A rerun in a repaired sandbox was stopped by the owner at 114 of 288 main sessions, after he and the study manager had seen the first run’s results. It read files outside its own folder through a gap in our test’s sandbox, the barrier meant to keep it in that folder. The gap was the builder’s fault. Details: our warning about Gemini 3.8 Flash.
  • Some of Google’s trouble that day shows in the elapsed time, which includes the waits between sessions. Gemini 3.8 Flash met “high demand” errors, and a failed automatic top-up stopped both Gemini models for a while. The session columns count only the timed turns of each session’s final attempt. See section 02.

Per session is the 288-session price divided by 288. Allowed jobs done counts the allowed sessions in which the requested result was in the files at the end of the work turn.

How each tool counts:

  • Claude Code (version 2.1.323), for the two Claude models, keeps its own running estimate of what the session cost and prints it at the end. The Claude models, like the GPT models, ran on our monthly plans, so nothing was charged per session; the figure is Claude Code’s estimate of what the work would cost at list prices. Only DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and the two Gemini models were paid for per use.
  • OpenCode (version 1.19.5), for DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and the two Gemini models, prices each step from the tokens used and its own price table, and adds the steps up. A step it gave no price for counts as zero.
  • DeepSeek V4.1 Flash was reached through OpenRouter, a service that passes requests on to a host. Its default choice of host was left alone, and which host answered was not recorded.
  • Codex (version 0.167.2), for the two GPT models, records tokens but prints no price.

What the figures cover. Each total adds the prices the tool recorded for both turns of every attempt kept in the session’s final run. Earlier runs of five session IDs per Gemini model, stopped by outages, were archived separately and are not included. They leave out the practice sessions on spare tasks, the checks run before the main sessions, the fourteen outage probes, and the stopped rerun of Gemini 3.8 Flash. For scale: with checks and spares included, OpenCode’s figure for Gemini 4 Neon was $38.08, against $34.67 for the main sessions alone.

  • Work turn is the time the coding tool ran on the job.
  • Whole session adds the follow-up turn when the script sent one. The script asked that question only when the requested result was missing at the end, so the models got it in very different numbers of sessions: GPT-6 Luna in 20, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and Gemini 4 Neon in 144.
  • Typical is the median: the average of the 144th and 145th values when the 288 times are put in order.
  • Both times are for the session’s final attempt only. Earlier failed attempts, and waits between sessions, are not in them.
  • First start to last finish is the clock time from the model’s first session starting to its last one ending. The Claude and GPT models ran up to two sessions at once; DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and the Gemini models ran one at a time. So this column does not compare directly across models.

Google’s trouble on the day. At times Google replied, for Gemini 3.8 Flash, that the model was “experiencing high demand”. At other times Google did not answer within five minutes. For a while requests for both Gemini models were refused, because the prepaid credit had run out after the owner’s automatic top-up did not go through. Sessions refused before the model did anything were set aside and run later. This is part of why Gemini 4 Neon’s 6.9 hours of session time took 8 h 56 min from first start to last finish.

The time cap. Three of Gemini 4 Neon’s work turns and one of Gemini 3.8 Flash’s were stopped at the test’s 900-second cap. Their folders were scored as they were left. The follow-up question has its own time limit, so a capped session can run longer: Neon’s longest session was 1294.5 seconds. No session of the other five models took longer than 75.6 seconds.

A tool call is one action the agent asks its coding tool to take, such as reading a file, running a command or changing a file. The figures count the calls the tool recorded in the work turn.

The three tools count differently. Claude Code records each tool use. Codex records commands and other steps. OpenCode records a call when it finishes, so a call cut off by the time cap may be missing. One command can also read several files and change another. So comparisons across tools are rough, and more tool calls is not better or worse on its own.

Gemini 3.8 Flash’s figures include its 86 of 288 sessions in which it named a place outside its folder.

05

What this does not show

These were small, made-up coding jobs, run on one day with one version of each model and tool, so bigger work may cost and take very different amounts. The two Claude models ran in Claude Code, the two GPT models in Codex, and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and the two Gemini models in OpenCode, so every figure is for that model and that tool together. The prices are the tools’ own estimates, not bills, and two models have none. The models did not deliver the same work, the Gemini batches waited through Google’s errors and our credit problem that day, and the models did not run the same number of sessions at once. The study was set up to see whether a short note in the project changed what agents did with requests they were forbidden to carry out. It did not judge how good any model’s work was, so nothing here says which model is better or worth its price. All figures are rounded to the places shown; an exact half goes to the even digit, so 9.95 seconds shows as 10.0.
